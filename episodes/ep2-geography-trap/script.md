## COLD OPEN

> The most important line dividing the world's great powers isn't drawn between democracies and dictatorships. You won't find it on any border crossing either. It's drawn by the map itself.
>
> Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin have met more than forty times — far more than either has met any other world leader. Their friendship is often explained by politics. But there's a deeper reason. Russia and China share the same problem: two giant countries, each locked inside its own geography, each searching for a way out.

## CHAPTER 1 — RUSSIA: A FORTRESS WITH NO WALLS

> Start with land. Russia and China each share a land border with fourteen countries — more than any other nation on Earth. Every one of those borders has to be watched, patrolled and, if necessary, defended. And that costs money that can't be spent on anything else.
>
> For Moscow, the most worrying stretch is in the west. Six of Russia's neighbours are now NATO members: Norway, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland. When Finland joined the alliance in 2023, it added more than thirteen hundred kilometres to that frontier on its own. Altogether, Russia now faces NATO across roughly two and a half thousand kilometres of land, from the Arctic to the Baltic.
>
> In the east, the picture is calmer. Russia finished settling its border with China in 2008, and its Asian neighbours are mostly friendly. But here's the catch: very few Russians live there. Around three quarters of the population, and most of the economy, sit west of the Ural Mountains. Moscow and Saint Petersburg alone produce more than a quarter of the country's output, while the enormous, resource-rich Far East adds only around five percent.
>
> So Russia's most valuable land is exactly where its most nervous border is. And that border has almost nothing to protect it.
>
> Look at Europe from orbit and the pattern jumps out. Yes, there are the Alps and the Carpathians. But between the Pyrenees and the Urals lies one vast, flat corridor — the European Plain. It is narrow near Poland and keeps widening the further east you go, until at the Urals it spans thousands of kilometres.
>
> For centuries, that open plain has been the road into Russia. The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth took Moscow in the early 1600s. Sweden marched in during the Great Northern War. Napoleon reached Moscow in 1812. Germany came twice in the twentieth century. Again and again, the danger arrived from the west, across land with nothing to stop it.
>
> During the Cold War, Moscow believed it had finally solved the problem. The Soviet Union and its satellite states pushed the front line all the way to the middle of Germany, and for decades NATO planners feared Soviet tank armies pouring through a narrow gap in the West.
>
> Then, in 1991, it all collapsed. In a few chaotic months, Russia lost a buffer it had spent centuries building, and its frontier fell back hundreds of kilometres to the east. In 2005, Vladimir Putin called the fall of the Soviet Union "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the century." Strategically, from Moscow's point of view, that was the logic: everything the empire had once held as a cushion was gone.
>
> Without mountains or rivers to do the work, Russia has had to defend that frontier with soldiers and money. Even before the war in Ukraine, it typically spent around three to four percent of its economy on the military — a burden it carried even through the hard years of the 1990s.
>
> The cheaper option, in the Kremlin's eyes, was influence: friendly governments as far west as possible, ideally reaching the Baltic in the north and the Carpathians in the south. Belarus stayed close. Ukraine, for a time, was led by leaders friendly to Moscow. But the line kept moving the other way. Poland joined NATO in 1999. Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania followed in 2004. And in 2014, Ukrainians drove out a president who had turned away from Europe.
>
> NATO is a defensive alliance, and no Western government had any interest in invading Russia. But Moscow reads its history through the map — and its rulers have reached for the same answer again and again: push the frontier outward until it reaches something defensible.
>
> After throwing off Mongol rule in the fifteenth century, Russia expanded east until it reached the Urals and beyond. Peter the Great fought his way to the Baltic. Catherine the Great helped carve up Poland. In 2022, Putin followed the same old instinct and launched the full-scale invasion of Ukraine to shorten and harden his western frontier.
>
> The price has been staggering. Western estimates of Russian casualties — killed and wounded — now run well past a million. ⚠ Russia has occupied roughly a fifth of Ukraine, but since 2022 its gains have often been measured in metres per day. ⚠ And military spending has swallowed around a third of Russia's federal budget. ⚠

## CHAPTER 2 — CHINA: SURROUNDED ON LAND

> Russia's closest partner is shaped by the same forces. For most of history, China was one of the richest civilisations on Earth, sitting at the eastern end of the Silk Road. Its problem was protecting that wealth — because almost every direction offered a threat.
>
> To the north there's no natural barrier, only the open Eurasian steppe. For two thousand years, nomadic horsemen rode south toward China's cities and rice fields. The Great Wall wasn't built for prestige. It was a hugely expensive answer to a geographic weakness. Later, the threat from the north was Russia itself — the two armies clashed along the Ussuri River as recently as 1969. Today, with Beijing and Moscow close partners, that front is quiet.
>
> To the southwest stands the opposite: the Himalayas, the strongest natural wall any country could hope for. But China and India have never agreed where the border actually runs. They fought a war over it in 1962, and their troops clashed again in the Galwan Valley in 2020.
>
> Why does China care so much about a frozen mountain line? Because of water. The Tibetan Plateau is often called the water tower of Asia. The Yangtze and Yellow rivers both begin there and flow down into China's heartland. The Yangtze basin alone grows around two-thirds of China's rice and produces roughly forty percent of its economy. ⚠
>
> The same plateau also feeds India: the Indus, the Brahmaputra and the Sutlej all start in Tibet. So each side sees the high ground as a matter of survival — and each fears the other holding it. China keeps a large standing force along the frontier. ⚠
>
> To the south lies a different danger: jungle. China's last full-scale war was in 1979, when it invaded Vietnam. Tens of thousands died on both sides in a few weeks, and the border barely moved. The war showed how hard it is to push an army through that terrain — and the Red River on the frontier is less of a barrier than it looks.
>
> Vietnam is no small adversary. By one widely used ranking, counting reserves and paramilitary forces, its total manpower is larger than China's. ⚠ And although both countries call themselves socialist, Hanoi has been moving closer to its old enemy, the United States, because of China's claims in the South China Sea. Washington has supplied patrol vessels and training aircraft, and American aircraft carriers have visited Da Nang. Vietnam still avoids formal alliances — but it has every reason to stay strong enough to worry Beijing. So China keeps troops and fortifications on that border too, more than two decades after the two countries signed their land-border treaty in 1999.
>
> Then there's the far northwest: Xinjiang. It's the gateway to Central Asia, and the route for Xi Jinping's plan to revive the old Silk Road trade. But it's a region where Chinese rule has long been contested. Large-scale Han Chinese settlement arrived only after 1949, encouraged by the new People's Republic, partly to secure the frontier with the Soviet Union.
>
> Tensions with the Uyghur population boiled over in Ürümqi in July 2009, when riots left nearly two hundred people dead. Under Xi Jinping, Beijing's response became mass detention and surveillance. The United States government, the UK Parliament and the French National Assembly have described China's treatment of the Uyghurs as genocide; Beijing rejects the charge and calls it counter-terrorism.
>
> Every one of these frontiers — steppe, mountains, jungle, desert — demands soldiers and money. And all of that spending on land leaves less for the threat that matters most to China today. That threat comes from the sea.

## CHAPTER 3 — THE MALACCA DILEMMA

> For most of its history, the Pacific was China's safe side. Then came the First Opium War in 1839, when the British Royal Navy shattered Chinese defences and forced the Qing dynasty to open its ports. The sea had become the gateway for invasion.
>
> Today, the danger is different. China has imported oil since 1993, and it now buys around seventy percent of the crude it uses from abroad, plus around forty percent of its natural gas. Most of that oil — around four-fifths of it — sails through a single passage: the Strait of Malacca.
>
> The strait runs nearly nine hundred kilometres between Malaysia and the Indonesian island of Sumatra, and at its narrowest point, near Singapore, the shipping channel is less than three kilometres wide. On one side sit India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands, home to a joint military command. On the other is Singapore, whose Changi naval base regularly hosts American warships.
>
> Chinese leaders have a name for this vulnerability: the Malacca Dilemma. And Malacca is only one of several chokepoints around China's coasts — the Miyako Strait near Japan, the Luzon Strait near the Philippines — each of them lined by islands that lean toward the United States.
>
> Beijing's first answer was to build around the problem. In 2013, in Kazakhstan, Xi Jinping announced what became the Belt and Road Initiative: a web of railways, roads, pipelines and ports to move trade over land, away from vulnerable sea lanes.
>
> Its flagship is the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor. The idea: tankers from the Persian Gulf unload at Gwadar in southwest Pakistan, and cargo travels north by road and rail, across Pakistan and into Xinjiang, never touching Malacca.
>
> China has poured tens of billions of dollars into it. ⚠ But the results have been thin. Gwadar handles a tiny fraction of the traffic of a major port. ⚠ Singapore alone moved more than forty-one million containers in 2024. And the overland route has been dangerous: separatist militants have repeatedly attacked Chinese workers in Pakistan, killing around twenty Chinese nationals in recent years. ⚠
>
> Even if every problem were solved, geography would still have the last word. Gwadar sits about 3,800 kilometres from Diego Garcia — the American and British base in the Indian Ocean — well within range of long-range bombers stationed there.
>
> The wider Belt and Road tells a similar story. Researchers at AidData have estimated that China's overseas lending reached well over a trillion dollars, and that most of it now goes to countries in financial distress. The projects have built real infrastructure — but they haven't redrawn the world's trade routes.
>
> So the old sea lanes still rule. And China — the world's biggest trading nation, with exports of around four trillion dollars and a trade surplus that topped one trillion in 2025 — depends on waters its rivals could close.
>
> That's why Beijing decided it had to become a naval power. US Navy intelligence has estimated China's shipbuilding capacity at more than two hundred times America's. China's navy is now the largest in the world by number of ships — more than 370 — and the Pentagon expects it to pass 430 by 2030.

## CHAPTER 4 — TAIWAN AND THE ISLAND CHAIN

> But more ships can't erase a chokepoint. In Beijing's thinking, there's only one permanent fix: Taiwan.
>
> Taiwan matters for many reasons — politics, history, and its world-leading chip industry. But geographically, it's the key in the lock. It sits at the centre of the First Island Chain, the string of islands from Japan to the Philippines that boxes in China's coast. Control Taiwan, and its eastern harbours would open straight onto the deep Pacific, giving China's navy room to manoeuvre far beyond its home waters.
>
> Yet even then, the oil would still pass through Malacca, and the US Navy and its partners would still be there. And numbers alone don't decide a naval contest. The American fleet is far heavier — its ships displace more than twice as much tonnage as China's. ⚠ Much of China's construction is frigates and patrol ships built for nearby waters, not the long-range power needed to break a blockade thousands of kilometres from home. In submarines, China is still widely judged to be behind the United States in key technologies.
>
> China is catching up. But an invasion of Taiwan would remain the riskiest gamble the Communist Party could take — one that could threaten its own hold on power.
>
> So why not build an even bigger navy, faster? Because China can't put everything into the sea. It still has to pay for all those land frontiers. And that is the dividing line between great powers: some are forced to be land powers. Others are free to become sea powers and dominate world trade.

## CHAPTER 5 — RUSSIA'S FROZEN COAST

> Russia shows just how trapped a land power can be at sea. It has one of the longest coastlines in the world — but the great majority of it faces the Arctic Ocean, frozen for much of the year. ⚠
>
> Ever since Peter the Great, Russian rulers have dreamed of a warm-water port with open access to the world. Murmansk in the far north stays ice-free thanks to the last warmth of the Gulf Stream, but it opens onto the Arctic. In the south, Novorossiysk and Sevastopol in occupied Crimea never freeze — but they sit inside the Black Sea.
>
> To reach the open ocean from there, a ship must pass the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles, both controlled by Turkey. Then it faces a choice: south through the Suez Canal and the narrow Bab-el-Mandeb at the mouth of the Red Sea, or west through the Strait of Gibraltar, overlooked by Spain and the British base at Gibraltar — both NATO members.
>
> In the Pacific, Vladivostok's harbour — the Golden Horn Bay — ices over in winter and needs icebreakers, and ships leaving the Sea of Japan must pass straits controlled by Japan.
>
> Russia tried other routes. In the nineteenth century it competed with Britain across Central Asia in the "Great Game," hoping to reach the Indian Ocean. It leased Port Arthur in Manchuria — only to lose it to Japan in 1905. Even at the height of Soviet power, this was the one geographic problem Moscow never solved.
>
> Now the Kremlin sees a new opportunity: a warming Arctic. Sea ice is shrinking fast, and scientists expect the Arctic Ocean to be practically ice-free in summer at least once before 2050. Russia controls roughly half of the Arctic coastline, and the Northern Sea Route along it can cut the voyage between northern Europe and East Asia by around forty percent compared with the Suez route.
>
> But the Arctic has only two doors. In the Pacific, ships must pass through the Bering Strait, between Russia and Alaska — where the United States keeps major air bases, including F-22 fighters at Joint Base Elmendorf–Richardson near Anchorage. Putin saw that base for himself in August 2025, when he met the US president there. Further south, Naval Base Kitsap in Washington State is home to aircraft carriers and the US Navy's Ohio-class ballistic-missile submarines.
>
> In the Atlantic, the other door is the gap between Greenland, Iceland and the United Kingdom — the GIUK Gap — one of the most closely watched stretches of sea during the Cold War, and once again a focus for NATO.
>
> And any ship heading for Rotterdam must pass between Norway's Svalbard archipelago and the Norwegian mainland. A 1920 treaty limits military use of Svalbard, but it recognises Norway's sovereignty, and Norway — a NATO founder — patrols those waters. With Finland and Sweden now in NATO, the alliance has an unbroken route to the far north, and it trains there regularly in large exercises such as Cold Response.
>
> So Russia faces a hard choice: spend on a stronger navy to guard the Arctic, or on the army that holds its long land frontiers. With a smaller economy than China's, it can't afford both.

## CHAPTER 6 — THE GEOGRAPHY OF POWER

> Russia keeps drawing the short straw. Its territory forces it to be a land power, not a sea power that can dominate trade and route around hostile chokepoints. A warmer Arctic may open new shipping lanes, but it can't move Russia to a better place on the map.
>
> China has become the world's second-largest economy, rich enough to launch warships at astonishing speed. But no amount of money can move an ocean, or raise mountains where there are none.
>
> Now compare the United States. Two oceans separate it from any serious rival. It shares land borders with only two countries — one shielded in part by the Rio Grande and deserts, the other a close ally. Its coasts open straight onto the world's sea lanes: no frozen harbours, no foreign-controlled straits. That freedom helped make it the richest country on Earth — rich enough to be something almost no nation in history has managed: a great land power and a great sea power at the same time.
>
> Russia and China want to reshape the world order. But geography means they start the race from behind — locked inside the map, watching the leader from the other side of the ocean.
